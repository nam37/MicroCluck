1Summary

MicroPup is a small four-legged walking robot dog, about 17 cm long. Its face is a Freenove ESP32-S3 3.5" touchscreen (FNK0104N) in a yellow bezel at the front of the body, running the animated face and voice of Neil's DeskSprite project. Behind it is a rounded white body on four aluminium legs, two Dynamixel XL330 servos per leg, with a paddle tail that wags.

Walking comes from a reinforcement-learning policy trained in simulation with the same tools as Pollen Robotics' Microduck (microduck_rl, mjlab, the BAM XL330 actuator model) and runs on the robot at 50 Hz. MicroPup is driven from a phone browser, talks through DeskSprite's voice relay, and has a camera from v1 for a first-person view and for its eyes to follow faces. Following people and patrolling are v2 (§11).

Three decisions shape everything else:

  • Three boards, one job each. The face board runs the face, voice, phone controller and behaviour. A second ESP32-S3, the body board, runs the walking loop, the servos, the IMU and every safety check. An ESP32-P4 vision board runs the camera and, in v2, person detection. The body never depends on the other two to stay safe.
  • Simulation first. The body, the walking policy and the firmware logic are proven in simulation before most parts are ordered (§3). The dog's walking and belly stand-up already pass there (§7).
  • Heavy compute off the robot. Speech goes through DeskSprite's relay on a VPS, and v2's person detection runs off the robot too. An on-board Raspberry Pi is added only if latency forces it.

MicroPup replaced the MicroCluck biped on 9 November 2026: four legs remove the biped's two hardest problems, getting up after a fall and the XL330's thermal margin. The repository keeps the MicroCluck name. It is a personal project and is not affiliated with Pollen Robotics, Hugging Face or Stanford.

2Goals and non-goals

v1 goals

IDGoal
G1Stand and walk on flat indoor floors from velocity commands: forward and backward up to 0.3 m/s, turning up to 1.0 rad/s, and both at once. No sideways steps (two servos per leg cannot make them).
G2Three boards with separate jobs (§5). The body board alone keeps the robot safe, whatever the face and vision boards do.
G3Phone control from any browser (iOS and Android), no app install, with a live camera view.
G4An expressive face from DeskSprite, mapped to the robot's state, with touch interaction and a tail that adds to the expression (§9.6).
G5Safe failure: dead-man stop, fall detection, servo protection, low-battery handling, E-stop, an obstacle stop from the ToF sensor.
G6OTA updates for all three boards; live telemetry for sim2real debugging.
G7Personality: IMU-stabilized eyes, eyes that follow a face the camera sees, a wagging tail, sounds, and voice conversation through DeskSprite's relay.
G8Get up from lying on its belly, and lie down on command. The robot's rest pose is lying down.
G9Prove the design, the walking policy and the body firmware's logic in simulation before buying the bulk of the parts.

v2 goals

  • Follow a person, using person detection off the robot (§11).
  • Patrol: wander with obstacle avoidance, recognise printed markers on doorways and a dock, and find its way back to the dock.
  • Voice commands that move it ("come here", "sit", "lie down") and answers about what the camera sees.

Non-goals for v1

  • Autonomous navigation of any kind (v2).
  • Sideways walking, and getting up from the back or a side. Both need a third servo per leg (hip abduction), which v1 does not have; a person rights a robot that has rolled over, and the robot asks for help (§7.5).
  • Rough terrain and stairs.
  • Running the walking policy on anything but the body board.
  • Any commercial use.

3Development approach

Work runs in two phases. Phase A builds MicroPup in simulation: the model, the walking policy, the design checks, the C port of the policy and a virtual MicroPup that runs the real body firmware against the simulator (§8). Phase B builds the robot. Much of Phase A is done: the dog's model, its walking policy and its belly stand-up all pass in simulation (§7.2).

The face board is already owned and already runs DeskSprite, so face and voice work continues in parallel. Servos ship with a lead time (§6.1), so they are ordered at the design freeze, once the remaining simulation checks are done: the forehead camera in the model, the shell's clearance to the legs over the gait, and a backlash-trained policy (§7.2). Driver bring-up on one or two early servos, the body board and the IMU may start earlier.

4Reference designs and constraints

MicroPup borrows from three places: Stanford's Pupper V3 for the idea of a small learned-gait dog, Pollen's Microduck for the training stack and actuator model, and DeskSprite for the face and voice.

  • Pupper V3's code is GPL-3.0. MicroPup takes ideas and published numbers from it, not code.
  • Pollen's microduck_rl is Apache-2.0. MicroPup uses its training recipe, tasks and BAM actuator model in a fork. None of Microduck's meshes or mechanical design is used, so their non-commercial licence does not apply to MicroPup's parts.
  • Actuator fidelity is the dominant sim2real gap at this scale. The XL330 is modelled down to its voltage control law, with randomized battery voltage, voltage sag, command delay and friction. Changing servo means identifying a new actuator model, which is why the XL330 stays (a Feetech HD1910M was considered and turned down on 9 November 2026).

XL330-M288-T key specs

18 g, 20 × 34 × 26 mm. Stall torque 0.52 N·m at 5 V (1.47 A) and 0.60 N·m at 6 V; the Current Limit register defaults to 1.75 A, so about 0.60 N·m is the ceiling. Input 3.7–6.0 V, 5.0 V recommended; Microduck runs it from a 2S pack and so does MicroPup (§6.4). TTL half-duplex bus, 3.3 V logic, 9.6 kbps to 4 Mbps, Protocol 2.0. Feedback includes position, velocity, current, temperature and input voltage. 12-bit absolute encoder. The estimated continuous torque is about 0.10 N·m, 20% of stall; MicroPup's walking runs near it (§7.2), so servo temperature is monitored (S4).

5System architecture

  • Face board. The FNK0104N runs DeskSprite's firmware, extended for MicroPup: the face, touch, voice through the relay, the phone controller page and WebSocket, and the behaviour layer that turns phone, voice and (in v2) autonomy into velocity commands and actions. It holds no safety duty: if it crashes, the body board stops and lies down.
  • Body board. A second ESP32-S3 owns everything that moves: the 50 Hz policy loop, the servo bus, the IMU, the battery monitor, the ToF sensor and the state machine. All safety checks live here (§9.8). It has Wi-Fi only for OTA and development, off while walking. This split is what future-proofs the robot: the face and vision boards can grow (voice, autonomy, more vision) without touching the loop's timing, and a third servo per leg (12 on the same bus) or a better IMU needs no change to the face board.
  • Vision board. An ESP32-P4 board (Waveshare ESP32-P4-WIFI6 or DFRobot FireBeetle 2, §6.2) in the front of the body runs the camera on the forehead: a hardware-encoded MJPEG stream for the phone's view, a face finder for the eyes' gaze, and in v2 person detection on the board itself. It does not command the legs; what it finds reaches the body only through the face board's behaviour layer, under the body's limits.

Vision board (ESP32-P4). 6.3Servo bus interface

  • The gate drives the bus only while DIR is high, then releases the line so servos can reply.
  • RX also hears the board's own transmissions. The dxl driver discards that echo, or the UART's RS-485 mode ignores RX during TX.
  • 3.3 V logic matches the XL330's TTL level. Unused gates: inputs and enables tied to GND.
  • Built on perfboard with DIP parts and a socket. No surface-mount soldering anywhere in the build.
  • Eight servos on one chain, the legs. The tail's micro servo is not on the bus: it takes a PWM pin and 5 V from the buck, so its stalls and current spikes stay off the servo rail and the 3.3 V logic.

6.4Power

  • Servo rail: the 2S pack directly, from about 6.6 V near empty to 8.4 V full, with no regulator. This is what Microduck does and what the training model assumes (6.5–8.2 V with load-dependent sag). It is above ROBOTIS's rated 3.7–6.0 V, an accepted risk (§12).
  • Servo settings for the higher voltage: the XL330's Max Voltage Limit tops out at 7.0 V, so a fresh pack sets the Input Voltage Error bit and the Alert flag. As on Microduck, Shutdown is set to 52 (not 53) so torque is not cut, and the dxl driver ignores that bit and the Alert flag in its health checks (S4).
  • Peak current: the Current Limit register caps each servo at 1.75 A, so the theoretical worst case with all eight stalled is about 14 A. Walking draws far less; measure it in H1, then size the fuse and the BMS's peak rating from the measurement. At least 1000 µF of bulk capacitance at the servo bus entry.
  • Logic rail: one 5 V buck, 3 A, from the pack into all three boards, grounds joined at one star point. It must accept the pack's full range and ride through the sag that servo current causes. Budget: face board with Wi-Fi and audio up to about 1 A peaks, vision board streaming and detecting about 0.3–0.5 A average with Wi-Fi peaks up to about 1.3 A, body board about 0.2 A. Peaks of the face and vision boards together stay under the buck's 3 A.
  • Monitoring: the INA226 on the pack gives voltage and current to the body board; the servos' Present Input Voltage register is a second gauge, averaged over about 10 s, with about 6.6 V as the empty floor (S5).
  • Charging: the charger and BMS hold the pack at or below 8.4 V. A charging dock is v2 (§11); its contacts and mass are reserved now.

6.5Mechanical

The simulation model (build_quad_model.py, §7.1) is the current design. OpenSCAD in hardware/ takes its numbers from it in H3; until then, the model is the source of truth for dimensions.

  • Body: a rounded box 170 mm long and 68 mm wide, edges rounded 7 mm and the ends 10 mm, the top sloping 16 mm down toward the tail. The four hip servos sit inside with their shafts out through the side walls, hips 120 mm apart front to rear. The battery lies in the belly between them and the electronics on a layer above, which slides fore and aft to set the balance (§7.2). White in the renders.
  • Face and bezel: the FNK0104N in landscape at the front, leaning back 10°, in a yellow bezel with soft (TPU) bumper lips that stand proud of the glass, so forward falls and belly-down landings meet the lips, not the screen. The bezel has a 14 mm chin below the screen and room above it for the camera.
  • Camera on the forehead: the camera module (about 25 × 24 mm, on its ribbon) centred in the bezel's top edge, with the vision board behind the bezel in the front of the body; about 170 mm above the floor standing, tilted 25–30° up from the face, with a lens of about 120° diagonal. That keeps a standing adult's face in view from about 0.5 m out, and the floor from about 0.45–0.6 m ahead. The chin position of the first model (about 105 mm up, looking 10° up) saw only legs and floor closer than about 2.7 m. A camera just above the screen also lines the on-screen eyes up with where the robot is looking.
  • ToF sensor in the chin: facing forward and slightly down, for obstacles and drops ahead. Its cable runs back to the body board.
  • Ears: two rounded lobes on the bezel's top corners. They double as bumpers and keep the forehead camera off the floor in a forward fall.
  • Legs: thigh 55 mm and shank 55 mm, hip pitch axis to knee axis and knee axis to foot. Aluminium flat bar, 1/2" × 1/8", with printed PETG cradles for the servos; the knee servo rides at the knee on the thigh. TPU ball feet, 16 mm across. Standing height is 100 mm from the hip axis to the foot centre. Not stainless mending plates, which weigh about 100 g each.
  • Tail: a paddle tail from the reference photo: a slim J-shaped stalk leaning back out of a ribbed boot, ending in a teardrop bulb, matte charcoal. It is driven by an SG90-class 9 g micro servo in the rear of the body, on PWM from the body board. Expression only: as a counterweight it moves the centre of mass by about 1.6 mm. Down by the floor it slows forward walking 5 to 10%, so the face layer keeps it up or wagging while walking.
  • IMU: rigidly mounted to the body frame near the hip midpoint. Its exact pose goes into the model.
  • Cabling: the servo chain runs along each side with strain relief across the hip; the knee servo's cable runs along the thigh. A dock contact pair under the chest is reserved for v2.

Mass budget. Estimates until each part is weighed (H4). The walking policy trains with the trunk's mass randomized from 0.95 to 1.15 times nominal, and the belly stand-up passes with 60 g extra, so v2 hardware up to about 60 g needs no retraining.

7Simulation and training

The full study, with every run's numbers, is quadruped-study.html. This section keeps what the rest of the document depends on.

7.1Model and task

  1. Fork. pollen-robotics/microduck_rl is forked; the dog lives on the branch microcluck_quad in the worktree ~/microcluck_quad, not yet pushed. It becomes the sim/ submodule.
  2. Model. src/mjlab_microduck/robot/quad/build_quad_model.py writes the robot, scene and get-up models from parameters: the 8-servo body and a 12-servo variant with hip abduction, tail and shell styles, ears, stance height (QUAD_STAND_H, 0.100 m), knee servo position and mass budget. Every process of a run (training, evaluation, video) must see the same environment variables.
  3. Collision naming. Only geoms named *_collision collide in the GPU simulator; anything else is switched off. Every leg part, the shell, the bezel and the tail are named for collision.
  4. Actuator order. Actuators are declared in joint order. The BAM actuator applies each joint's torque by index, so a mismatch drives the wrong motor (it once made the tail look able to flip the robot). quad_getup_search.py asserts it.
  5. Task. Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroCluck-Quad8, Pollen's velocity recipe with: no head or body command slots, a sideways command kept alive at ±0.001 m/s and never tracked, a bucket of pure backward commands, tighter tracking at slow speeds, forward commands capped at 0.5 m/s, a staged torque penalty and the full Pollen randomization plus trunk mass 0.95–1.15 and centre of mass ±15 mm.
  6. Observation contract v1 (33 values): gyro (3), projected gravity (3), joint positions (8), joint velocities (8), last action (8), twist command (3). Actions are 8 joint targets. The tail is not in the policy; the face layer drives it, and the body board limits it while walking.
  7. Actor network: 512-256-128 with ELU, about 182,000 parameters or 0.73 MB as float32. A 256-128-64 actor (about 50,000) is the ablation if inference on the body board is too slow (§9.3).

7.2Design validation (S3)

7.3Policy export pipeline

Lives in tools/policy_export/.

  1. Input is the ONNX file from the fork's export script, with the normalizer baked in. Never convert a checkpoint by hand.
  2. Extract weights, biases, activations and the normalizer; fold the normalizer into the first layer: W' = W / σ and b' = b − W·(μ/σ).
  3. Emit policy_<name>.h with C arrays and metadata: input and output sizes, action scale, default pose and the contract hash.
  4. Generate golden vectors: at least 1000 plausible observations and their ONNX outputs.
  5. Device test: C inference on the body board against the golden vectors, maximum absolute error ≤ 1e-4.
  6. Closed-loop test: the same C inference, built for the host, drives MuJoCo with the same gait as the ONNX policy.

7.4Sim2real contract

docs/sim2real-contract.md is the single, versioned, hashed source of truth for the body board and the simulation:

  • Observation order, units and scaling (the 33 values of §7.1)
  • Where joint velocity comes from: the servo's velocity register, read in the same fast sync read as position (Pollen's choice)
  • Action post-processing: action scale, default pose offset, clipping, and whether a low-pass is applied (Pollen's runtime filters, their training does not; settle it before H5)
  • Servo control mode and firmware P gain (Pollen: position P 200, I and D zero), since the simulation emulates the servo's own loop
  • Command ranges the face board's commands are clamped to: forward −0.3 to +0.3 m/s, turning ±1.0 rad/s, sideways zero
  • Loop rate (50 Hz) and expected command delay

The body board refuses to enable torque if the policy's contract hash does not match its build (S7).

7.5Getting up

  • From the belly: a scripted push-up, 0.5–1.2 s, from lying belly-down with the legs along the sides. It passes every stress tested (§7.2) and needs about 0.1 N·m per servo, so strength is not a concern. It is v1's normal way to stand: the robot rests lying down.
  • From the back or a side: not possible with two servos per leg. The legs swing only fore and aft and no motion found got the body past lying on its side, with a box or a rounded shell, and the tail cannot flip it. It should only happen if the robot is kicked; it detects it (S3), goes limp, shows a help face and asks for help. A third servo per leg is the likely fix if it ever matters; the 12-servo model exists and is untrained.

8Virtual MicroPup

The virtual MicroPup runs the real body firmware's core against the simulator on the PC. A link emulator stands in for the face board, so the phone page, the face board's behaviour layer or a test script can drive it exactly as they would the robot. It is the regression harness for every safety requirement.

8.1Hardware abstraction layer

The body firmware's core (control loop, state machine, safety, policy inference, link protocol) is plain C with no ESP-IDF includes. It reaches the world only through this interface, with one implementation for the body board and one for the host.

8.2Host twin and fidelity

  • A Python harness runs MuJoCo with the MicroPup model and the same BAM actuator model as training, loads the body core as a shared library and steps it at 50 Hz of simulated time.
  • The link emulator speaks the §9.4 protocol over a local socket. A thin bridge serves the phone page from the PC, so the phone drives the simulated dog; DeskSprite's browser face can show the face from the same telemetry.
  • Servo positions are quantized like the 12-bit encoder; bus latency, command delay, IMU noise and bias, and ToF readings from ray casts are modelled. Battery voltage, link drops, pushes, obstacles and a stalled control task can be injected.
  • The twin writes the same log format as the robot, has a deterministic mode with fixed seeds, and runs headless for automated tests.

9.4Face–body link

A 3.3 V UART between the face board's UART header and the body board, plus ground, at 921,600 baud. Binary frames, COBS-framed with a CRC-16 and a sequence number; a bad frame is dropped and counted.

The body clamps every command to the contract's ranges (S6). It treats the link like a dead-man: no valid cmd for 300 ms sets velocity to zero, and no frame at all for 2 s lies the robot down (S2). The face board does the same for the phone (§9.5), so either break stops the robot.

9.6Face, voice, sound, touch and tail

Face. DeskSprite's SVG-derived face families and palettes on the landscape screen; the default is cyan, rounded eyes on black, as in the renders. DeskSprite already has listening, thinking, speaking, idle, happy, sad, surprise, love, sleepy and warning, with blinks and glances. MicroPup maps robot state onto them and adds a few:

WalkingHappy, gaze leading into the turn

Stabilized eyes and gaze. The face shifts opposite to the body's roll and pitch from the body board's gravity vector, low-pass filtered and clamped to about ±20 px; off when fallen or E-stopped. On top of that, the eyes look toward the face the vision board reports. Only the face's bounding box redraws, at no more than 30 fps.

Voice. DeskSprite's push-to-talk conversation through the VPS relay, unchanged. In v1 the robot talks but voice does not move it; v1.1 adds relay tools that map phrases ("lie down", "come here") to the same actions the phone sends, under the same body limits.

Sound. Short clips through the face board's codec: a happy yip when it starts walking, a startled one on a bump, a whine when it needs help, a sleepy sigh on low battery. Rate-limited; volume and mute in /settings.

Touch. A tap is a pet (love, wag). A long press is an E-stop.

Tail. The face board picks the tail's pose or wag (wag on happy and love, low on sleepy, still when fallen); the body board drives its micro servo by PWM, limits it while walking (kept up, small amplitude) and stops the PWM in E-stop. It has no position or temperature feedback.

9.7Mode state machine (body board)

STAND_UP eases the legs to the lying pose (along the sides) at low gains, then runs the scripted push-up of §7.5 and hands over to the policy. LIE_DOWN is its reverse, ending belly-down with the legs along the sides, then torque off. Both are joint-target trajectories, so v1 needs no extra training for them. Stand only when upright: belly-down or standing within 20° of level by the gravity vector; anything else is refused with a reason (S10).

9.8Safety requirements

All of them are enforced on the body board.

IDRequirement
S1E-stop from the phone, a long press on the screen or an estop frame turns torque off within one control cycle and latches until cleared.
S2Dead-man: 300 ms without a valid command sets velocity to zero; 2 s without any link frame lies the robot down.
S3Fall detection per §9.7, going limp to protect the gears and the screen.
S4Servo health: round-robin reads of temperature and hardware error status on all eight leg servos. Over the temperature threshold, stop and lie down to rest; on a hardware error, including a Bus Watchdog error (S9), torque off and report. The Input Voltage Error bit and its Alert flag are ignored (§6.4).
S5Low battery: warn, then lie down and turn torque off before the BMS cuts out; floor about 6.6 V, smoothed over about 10 s.
S6Commands are clamped to the policy's training ranges, whatever the face board sends.
S7A policy whose contract hash does not match the build cannot enable torque.
S8S1–S7, S10, S11 and the software side of S9 are covered by automated tests on the host twin.
S9Hang protection: a task watchdog on control_task and imu_task resets the board. With torque on, every servo's Bus Watchdog (register 98, 20 ms units) is about 100 ms, so a hung body board leaves the servos stopped; H1 measures what torque does afterwards. After any reset, the first bus action clears the Bus Watchdog and broadcasts torque off. The power switch stays reachable.
S10Stand only when upright (belly-down or standing, within 20° of level). The refusal and its reason reach the face and the phone.
S11Obstacle stop: with an obstacle closer than about 15 cm ahead in the ToF zones, forward velocity is clamped to zero; backing away and turning stay allowed. The face shows it.

9.9Vision in v1

  • Camera view: the vision board serves an MJPEG stream (QVGA to VGA, 10–15 fps) that the controller page shows behind the joystick.
  • Eye contact: the vision board runs a small face finder (esp-dl's human-face detector or similar, a few frames per second) and sends the largest face's position to the face board as small UDP packets on the home network (ESP-NOW if that adds lag). The face board turns it into gaze.
  • Nothing from vision moves the legs in v1.

11v2 autonomy

  • Following people first, detected on the robot. The vision board runs Espressif's pedestrian detector (esp-dl, 224 × 224 input). Espressif's table gives about 50 ms a frame on an ESP32-P4 at 400 MHz, about 20 fps; this board runs at 360 MHz, and streaming video at the same time costs some of that, so expect 10–15 fps and measure it in V0. It sends the target's bearing and size to the face board, whose behaviour layer turns them into velocity and turn commands, the same commands the phone sends, and the body board applies its limits and the obstacle stop. Detection on the robot works the same at home and away, needs no server or network round trip, and needs no new hardware.
  • Patrolling second. Wandering with ToF obstacle avoidance, plus AprilTag-style markers on doorways and on a charging dock, so the robot knows roughly where it is and can return to charge. A dock with contacts under the chest; the charger and BMS handle the pack.
  • Conversation with sight. "What do you see?" sends a frame through the relay to a vision-capable model.
  • Off-board vision, optional. For heavier models than the board can run (recognising family members or objects, a better person tracker) the vision board can send frames through the relay to a vision service on the VPS, in its own container next to the relay. The VPS (trinsicstudios-1, Ubuntu 24.04) has 6 vCPUs, 8 GB of RAM and 100 GB of disk, and no GPU, shared with the relay (limited to one CPU and 256 MiB) and the server's other services. A nano-class CPU detector (a YOLO-nano or MediaPipe person model under ONNX Runtime, at about 320 px) should manage 5–10 fps for one robot in a container limited to about 2 CPUs and 1 GiB; measure it before relying on it. The PC's GPU is the fallback if it falls short. An on-board Raspberry Pi is no longer expected: the P4 does the time-critical detection on the robot.
  • Possible future option: a home vision server on Neil's Raspberry Pi 4, for the same off-board work. Not on the robot (about 46 g bare and 3–6 W, too big for the 68 mm body and too hungry for the pack), but on the home network, always on, running the same vision container as the VPS. A Pi 4's CPU should manage roughly 3–8 fps with a nano-class detector at about 320 px; measure it. At home the frames never leave the house, so the round trip is shorter than through the VPS. Away from home the robot cannot reach a server inside the house directly: the ESP32 boards have no Tailscale client, so the robot keeps talking only to the public VPS, and the VPS forwards frames to the Pi over a Tailscale tailnet (VPS and Pi both joined). That path is longer than running detection on the VPS itself, so away from home the VPS would normally do it and the Pi is for home use or when the VPS is busy. The robot's firmware sees one endpoint, the relay, either way; the relay chooses where detection runs.
  • Hip abduction (12 servos) if following needs sideways steps or self-righting turns out to matter; the body board's bus and the model are ready for it.

13Open questions

  1. Where the face firmware lives: a MicroPup build inside DeskSprite's repository (recommended, so the face and voice stay one codebase), or a copy in this repository.
  2. Which ESP32-P4 board goes in the robot: the Waveshare ESP32-P4-WIFI6 (bring-up board, audio on board, larger) or the DFRobot FireBeetle 2 (25.4 × 60 mm, 360 MHz), after V0.

15Notes for Claude Code

  • MicroPup is the target; the biped is archived. Don't revive biped work unless Neil asks.
  • Work milestone by milestone and don't start the next until the exit criteria are met (§10).
  • Code in firmware/body/components/core never includes ESP-IDF headers; it reaches hardware only through the HAL (§8.1), and every core change must build and pass tests on the twin.
  • All safety lives on the body board. The face and vision boards may only ask; the body clamps and decides.
  • docs/sim2real-contract.md and docs/link-protocol.md are the sources of truth for their interfaces; firmware, twin and simulation reference the same hashes.
  • Never hand-edit generated policy headers; regenerate them with tools/policy_export.
  • In the simulation: every process of a run uses the same QUAD_* environment variables; only *_collision geoms collide; actuators are declared in joint order; an N-iteration rsl_rl run ends with model_(N-1).pt.
  • The sim repository's origin is Pollen's; never push to it.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop steps (wiring, soldering, flashing, stand tests, weighing parts) are manual. Flag them for Neil rather than assuming results.
  • Record significant decisions as ADRs in docs/decisions/.
