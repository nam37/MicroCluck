1Summary

MicroPup is a small four-legged walking robot dog, about 18 cm long and about 550 g, built to cost well under a ready-made robot. Its face is a 1.69" screen (240 × 280, portrait) in a yellow bezel at the front of the body, with a camera on a strip above it and a distance sensor in the chin. Behind it is a rounded white body with a sloped top on four legs, three serial bus servos per leg (abduction, hip, knee), the small 3.5 kg·cm servo MangDang makes for Mini Pupper 2, with a paddle tail on a micro servo.

Walking comes from a reinforcement-learning policy trained in simulation with the tools of Pollen Robotics' Microduck (microduck_rl, mjlab), with a model of the hobby servo in place of Pollen's XL330 model, and runs on the robot at 50 Hz. The servos report their own positions over the bus for the policy. A scripted trot is the fallback. MicroPup is driven from a phone browser, talks through DeskSprite's voice relay, and shows a first-person camera view. Following people and patrolling are v2 (§11).

Four decisions shape everything else:

  • Cost first. About $340–390 for everything with twelve servos, just below a $399 Microduck. The servos are about $164 of it with shipping; everything else comes from Amazon and Adafruit where they stock it. The third servo per leg was chosen for movement that looks alive (§7.2), not for walking. Anything that pushes the total toward the Microduck's price has to earn its place.
  • One board. A Waveshare ESP32-P4 board runs the walking loop, the safety checks, the face, voice, camera and Wi-Fi. The walking loop and safety get one core to themselves (§9.2).
  • Small bus servos, measured. The bus servo reports its position but has no published model and few published specs. It is simulated as an MG92B (same listed torque) for now and measured on the bench as soon as it arrives (H0, §10).
  • Simulation first, as before: the body, the walking policy and the firmware's logic are proven in simulation before the robot walks.

MicroPup replaced the MicroCluck biped on 9 November 2026, was redesigned for cost on 10 November 2026 and moved to twelve servos on the braced leg on 11 November 2026. The repository keeps the MicroCluck name. It is a personal project and is not affiliated with Pollen Robotics, Hugging Face, Stanford or Waveshare.

2Goals and non-goals

v1 goals

IDGoal
G1Stand and walk on flat indoor floors from velocity commands: forward and backward, turning, and both at once. The speed targets are set from the simulation (§7.2); at least 0.15 m/s forward is the bar. No sideways steps.
G2A bill of materials of about $340–390 for everything, including spares and a battery charger (twelve servos; it was $150–195 with eight).
G3Phone control from any browser (iOS and Android), no app install, with a live camera view.
G4An expressive face in DeskSprite's style on the 1.69" screen, mapped to the robot's state, with a tail that adds to the expression (§9.5).
G5Safe failure: dead-man stop, fall detection, servo protection without load feedback, low-battery handling, E-stop, an obstacle stop and a table-edge stop from the chin sensor.
G6OTA updates; live telemetry for sim2real debugging.
G7Personality: IMU-stabilized eyes, eyes that follow a face the camera sees, a wagging tail, sounds, and voice conversation through DeskSprite's relay.
G8Get up from lying on its belly, and lie down on command. The rest pose is lying down.
G9Prove the design, the walking policy and the firmware's logic in simulation before the robot walks.

v2 goals

  • Follow a person, with person detection on the P4 (§11).
  • Patrol: wander with obstacle avoidance using the chin sensor's 8 × 8 zones, recognise printed markers on doorways and a dock.
  • Voice commands that move it ("come here", "sit", "lie down").

Non-goals for v1

  • Autonomous navigation of any kind (v2).
  • Sideways walking, and getting up from the back or a side; a person rights a robot that has rolled over. The abduction servos may make both possible later.
  • Rough terrain, stairs, carrying a payload.
  • Long walks: hobby servos and a small battery give short sessions with rests (§6.4, S4).
  • Any commercial use.

3Development approach

Phase A builds MicroPup in simulation: the model, the walking policy, the design checks, the C port of the policy and the virtual MicroPup (§8). Phase B builds the robot, starting with a bench measurement of the servo (H0) that replaces the datasheet servo model. Because the parts are cheap and in stock, bench bring-up can start as soon as they arrive, alongside Phase A.

Purchase plan

Parts come from Amazon and Adafruit wherever they stock them: US stock, delivery in days, and returns that work. Two exceptions: the servos come from MangDang directly (MangDang store, flat $25 shipping, several weeks), and the VL53L5CX from SparkFun, Pololu or DigiKey. The servos are ordered first because they take longest; the CAD, the simulation and the firmware's servo bus are prepared while they travel, against the Amazon and Adafruit parts, which arrive within days. The abduction servos are fitted last: the first walk uses only the hips and knees (§7.2), so the robot can be built and walk with printed blocks in the four abduction mounts, and get its sway and lean when the servos go in.

4Reference designs and constraints

MicroPup takes the idea of a small learned-gait dog from Stanford's Pupper V3, the training stack from Pollen's Microduck, and the face, voice and personality from Neil's DeskSprite. Small commercial robot dogs of about this size and weight walk on hobby-class servos, which is the evidence that the servo class can do it.

Servo key specs

MangDang TTL serial bus servo (the leg servo since 11 November 2026, $9.90 at the MangDang store): listed at 3.5 kg·cm with position feedback, 4.8–7.4 V, ABS case. MangDang's Apache-2.0 board code for Mini Pupper 2 (mini_pupper_2_bsp, ESP-IDF) drives twelve of them from an ESP32 on one half-duplex serial line at 500 kbaud with Feetech's SCS protocol: a sync write sets all targets, and each servo's position is read back by its ID. Size, weight, speed, gear material, spline and position resolution are not published; they are measured on arrival (H0).

MG92B (the fallback, and the servo the simulation models): about 13.8 g, metal gears, stall torque about 3.5 kg·cm (0.343 N·m) at 6 V, about the MG90S's speed and the same mounting class, $11.95 at Adafruit. Position by pulse width like the MG90S below; read-back needs its potentiometer wired out.

MG90S (the earlier choice): about 13.4 g, 22.8 × 12.2 × 28.5 mm, metal gears. Stall torque about 1.8 kg·cm at 4.8 V and 2.2 kg·cm (0.216 N·m) at 6 V; about 0.10 s/60° at 4.8 V. Input 4.8–6.0 V. Position command by pulse width, about 1000–2000 µs over about 180°, at a 50 Hz frame. No position, speed, load or temperature feedback; the potentiometer inside can be wired out for a position reading (§6.3). Clones vary widely in torque, deadband and centring, so buy from one reputable seller and measure them (H0).

5System architecture

  • Core 1 owns everything that moves: the 50 Hz policy loop, the servo bus, the IMU, the chin sensor, the power monitor and the state machine. All safety checks live here (§9.7).
  • Core 0 runs everything else: the face on the screen, voice through the relay, the phone page and WebSocket, the camera stream and face finder, and the behaviour layer that turns phone, voice and (in v2) autonomy into velocity commands. It can only ask: commands pass to core 1 through a queue, and core 1 clamps them.
  • Why one board. The P4 is fast enough for all of it, and one board removes the second and third boards, the link between them and their wiring, which was much of the old design's cost and complexity. The price is that a crash anywhere resets everything; the servo outputs are wired to go limp on any reset (S9).

6.3Servo drive

  • One serial bus at 500 kbaud, half-duplex, through the bus adapter, in Feetech's SCS protocol as in MangDang's board code. Each control cycle one sync write sets all twelve targets, and each servo's position is read back by its ID. The IDs (1–12 in joint order: FL abduction, hip, knee, then FR, RL, RR) are set once in H0 over the adapter's USB, in software.
  • Zero and direction for each joint are calibrated in H0 and stored on the board. The position resolution is measured in H0; training rounds its targets to the real step (it uses the PWM step, 0.44°, until then).
  • Power off from reset: a bus servo keeps holding its last target when the controller resets or hangs, unlike a PWM servo whose pulses stop. So the servo rail runs through a power switch whose enable is pulled low: from power-on and after any reset the servos are unpowered and limp until core 1 switches them on (S9), and E-stop cuts the rail (S1). Ordinary limp states use the servos' torque-off command.
  • Read-back: positions come from the servos' own sensors. Their noise and bias are measured in H0 and set in training (it assumes ±0.02 rad noise and ±0.05 rad bias per joint until then). The scripted fallback gait needs no read-back.
  • Tail: the SG90 runs from one of the board's PWM pins at 50 Hz.

6.4Power

  • Servo rail: 6–7 V from a 5 A regulator off the 2S pack (a full pack's 8.4 V is over the servos' 7.4 V), through the servo power switch. Twelve servos near stall could draw more than the regulator's 5 A; measure walking current in H1, size the fuse from it and move to a larger regulator if needed. At least 470 µF of bulk capacitance where the rail meets the servo bus.
  • Logic rail: a 5 V, 2 A buck into the board. The P4 streaming video with Wi-Fi peaks at about 1.3 A.
  • Monitoring: the INA226 measures the pack voltage and the servo rail's current (S4, S5).
  • Runtime: a 450 mAh pack at an assumed 1.5–3 A average while walking (twelve servos, a heavier body) gives roughly 10–15 minutes of walking, longer idling. Measure it in H5; a bigger pack fits if the mass budget allows (§13).
  • Charging: off the robot with a balance charger. A charging dock is v2.

6.5Mechanical

The leg is drawn around the MG92B's case until the bus servo's real size is measured (H0); its servo pocket, tabs and horn then change. The parts are written as code with build123d in cad/ (cad/README.md): leg_braced.py for the leg, body.py for the body, check_fit.py and body_fit() for collisions over the range of motion, fit coupons before the real parts. The simulation model (build_quad_model.py with QUAD_SERVO=mg92b QUAD_DESIGN=micro QUAD_LEG=braced, §7.1) takes its masses and collision shapes from the CAD.

  • Body: the simulation's shape: about 180 mm long and 46 mm wide, 63 mm tall, the top sloping down toward the tail (10 mm drop at the rear), a tapered belly, 1.2 mm PLA or PETG walls (54 g). Hips 110 mm apart front to rear. The four abduction servos sit inside, their axes 36 mm out from the middle. The battery (50 × 30 × 13 mm stand-in) lies in the belly and the electronics (60 × 40 × 12 mm stand-in) above it. Side openings are cut where the shoulders sweep; the only notches left are about 8 mm for the hip bracket's inner arm.
  • Face: the 1.69" module (about 33 × 40 mm) in portrait in a yellow bezel with soft lips proud of the glass. The whole front of the body leans back 10° with the face, so the bezel sits flush.
  • Camera on the forehead: on a strip of the bezel above the screen, tilted about 25° up, so it sees people's faces rather than their feet; a wide lens if the kit's camera is too narrow (§13). The camera ribbon runs back to the board.
  • VL53L5CX in the chin: looking ahead and slightly down, so its upper zones see obstacles and its lower zones see the floor ahead (S11, S12). A plain opening or ST's recommended cover glass, to avoid reflections.
  • Legs (the braced leg, after Pollen's MicroDuck): thigh 45 mm and shank 45 mm, axis to axis and knee to foot; standing height 75 mm from the hip axis to the foot centre (82 mm trunk height).
    • The hip and knee servos lie along the thigh, clamped between a printed shell and a rigidity plate, so their cases are part of the structure. Each joint is held on both sides: a horn on one side, a 693 bearing on a pin on the other.
    • A U-shaped hip bracket around the top of the thigh hangs from the abduction servo; abduction ±29°, hip 70° back to 80° forward of home, knee 0–150°, with no collisions in any of the 27 test poses, front and rear.
    • The shank is a "heel fork": two plates from the knee reach back to a rod 19 mm behind it, and a 6 mm bar runs from the rod around the back of the knee to the foot. The leg tapers toward the foot, so it can lean 58° sideways when standing before anything but the foot touches a flat floor.
    • A curved shoulder cover (1.2 mm, 4.5 g) on each bracket covers the top and front of the joint between leg and body.
    • 43.6 mm wide per leg with the bus servo; about 135 mm across the shoulders, feet about 92 mm apart. Moving printed parts about 32 g per leg, plus three 693s and screws. The feet are bought 1/2" (12.7 mm) nitrile rubber balls snapped into a cup on the shank, which wraps them at the sides only, so the leg can lean 39–52° before the cup touches the floor.
  • Tail: the paddle tail on an SG90 at the rear, driven by a short pushrod so the servo sits forward of the sloping rear. Expression only.
  • IMU: rigidly mounted near the hip midpoint; its pose goes into the model.

Mass budget. From the simulation model, which takes its part masses from the CAD; estimates until each part is weighed (H4). Training randomizes the trunk's mass from 0.95 to 1.15 times nominal.

7Simulation and training

Every run's numbers are in the study, quadruped-study.html. This section keeps what the rest of the document depends on.

7.1Model and task

  1. Fork. pollen-robotics/microduck_rl is forked; the dog lives on the branch microcluck_quad in the worktree ~/microcluck_quad, not pushed. It becomes the sim/ submodule.
  2. Model. src/mjlab_microduck/robot/quad/build_quad_model.py. QUAD_SERVO=mg92b picks the servo and scales the body around it (mg90s for the earlier runs); QUAD_LEG=braced builds the braced leg with the CAD's masses and collision shapes for every part that can touch the floor (files robot_quad12_braced2_mg92b_micro*.xml); QUAD_DESIGN=micro adds the 1.69" face, the one-board electronics and the small battery (files robot_quad8_mg90s_micro*.xml). Every process of a run (training, evaluation, video) must see the same environment variables. The first micro runs have the camera in the chin; it moves to the forehead in the next model (a few grams, not expected to change the results).
  3. Servo model. HobbyServoActuator (src/mjlab_microduck/actuator/servo_models.py, parameters in robot/quad/params/): a DC-motor model from the datasheet (MG92B stall 0.343 N·m at 6 V, MG90S 0.216 N·m; no-load about 13 rad/s), a ±0.5° deadband and a proportional position loop. It is an estimate, replaced by a fitted model after H0. The bus servo is simulated as the MG92B (same listed torque) until then. Supply 5.6–6.0 V with sag, command delay 2–5 steps.
  4. Feedback. QUAD_FEEDBACK=pot gives the policy servo-grade joint angles (now the bus servos' own readings) (±0.02 rad noise, ±0.05 rad bias per joint, no joint velocities); blind gives it no joint feedback at all.
  5. Collision naming and actuator order as before: only *_collision geoms collide in the GPU simulator, and actuators are declared in joint order.
  6. Task. Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroCluck-Quad12 for the 12-servo dog (…-Quad8 for eight), Pollen's velocity recipe adapted for the dog: no sideways tracking, a bucket of backward commands, tighter tracking at slow speeds, a staged torque penalty, Pollen's randomization plus trunk mass 0.95–1.15 and centre of mass ±15 mm.
  7. Servo contract (tasks/quad_servo_action.py, after Mini Pupper 4's, on by default for the braced leg): the policy's output is clipped to ±1, scaled per joint, held in a band around home (abduction ±29° or less, hip and knee ±60°), rate-limited to 15° per 20 ms step and rounded to the PWM step; the policy sees the target that was really sent. Training ends an episode when any part but a foot touches the floor. QUAD_ABD_BAND, QUAD_ABD_COST, QUAD_PWM_TICK_DEG, QUAD_VY_MAX and QUAD_HISTORY set the study's variants.
  8. Observation contract v2: gyro (3), projected gravity (3), joint positions (12, pot only, optionally with 4 frames of history), last applied action (12), twist command (3): 33 values with pot read-back for twelve servos (25 for eight). Actions are 12 joint targets. The tail is not in the policy.

Decision: twelve servos in hardware, the first walk on the hips and knees. The abduction servo is there for looks: roll, sway, weight shift and leaning into turns, mostly while standing or idling, which a two-servo leg cannot do. The first walking policy uses the hip and knee servos, with abduction centred (b5) or allowed small corrections (b6), whichever does better on falls, pushes and abduction load. Result: b6, abduction within ±6° (2.2° RMS in the walk), tracks and turns close to the free b3 walk while loading the abduction servos half as much. A looser walk that uses abduction a little, enough to look less mechanical, can be trained later on the same hardware. Background. On 9 November an MG90S dog built around the old design (414 g, with the 60 g FNK0104N face board) stood and got up from its belly every time but barely walked (at most 0.06 m/s), with every joint at its stall torque. The micro design takes about 100 g out of the body to give the servos that margin back. A Feetech STS3215 dog was also tried and rejected (study §8).

What the result means. The servos can walk this body; training just did not find how. Standing (0.02 N·m) and belly stand-up (about 0.1 N·m) are well inside the servo, and a hand-written trot (hip ±0.20 rad, knee lift 0.40 rad, diagonal pairs half a cycle apart, 2.5 Hz) walks at 0.16 m/s, steers by shortening the stride on one side, and survives the play pushes. v1 therefore walks with a scripted gait: the walk cycle is played as joint targets, the IMU is used for falls and later for balance corrections, and no neural network runs on the body. The MG90S model is still a datasheet estimate, so H0 (bench measurement) stays first. A stronger servo, the TowerPro MG92B (0.343 N·m stall, same speed, 13.8 g, about $8–12), was simulated next; see below.

7.3Policy export pipeline

Lives in tools/policy_export/, unchanged from the earlier design: ONNX with the normalizer baked in, folded into the first layer, emitted as a C header with metadata and the contract hash, golden vectors checked on the board (maximum error ≤ 1e-4), and a closed-loop host test driving MuJoCo.

7.4Sim2real contract

docs/sim2real-contract.md is the single, versioned, hashed source of truth for the firmware and the simulation: observation order, units and scaling (§7.1); where joint angles come from (pot read-back or none); action post-processing and how a joint target becomes a pulse width (per-servo zero, direction and scale from H0); command ranges; loop rate and expected delay. The firmware refuses to enable the servos if the policy's contract hash does not match its build (S7).

7.5Getting up

  • From the belly: a scripted push-up from lying belly-down with the legs along the sides, as in the earlier design. It is the normal way to stand; the robot rests lying down.
  • From the back or a side: not attempted in v1 (the abduction servos may make it possible later). The robot detects it (S3), goes limp, shows a help face and asks for help.

8Virtual MicroPup

The virtual MicroPup runs the real firmware's control core against the simulator on the PC, so the phone page or a test script drives the simulated dog exactly as it would the robot. It is the regression harness for every safety requirement.

Hardware abstraction layer. The control core (control loop, state machine, safety, policy inference, command handling) is plain C with no ESP-IDF includes. It reaches the world through: servo outputs (pulse widths, enable and disable), joint angles (pot read-back, if fitted), IMU (gyro and gravity), clock (50 Hz tick, sim time on the host), range (the chin sensor's zones), power (pack voltage and servo current) and the command queue. The host twin runs MuJoCo with the same servo model as training, quantizes targets and readings like the servo bus, models command and read-back delay, IMU noise and ToF ray casts, and can inject low battery, pushes, obstacles, table edges and a stalled control task. It writes the robot's log format and runs headless for automated tests.

9Firmware

9.1One firmware, rewritten

ESP-IDF v5.x in C, FreeRTOS, on the ESP32-P4 (the P4's camera, hardware JPEG and AI functions need ESP-IDF). LVGL for the face, Espressif's MIPI camera stack and hardware JPEG encoder for the stream, esp-dl's face and pedestrian detectors, Wi-Fi through esp_hosted on the ESP32-C6. Updates over the air with A/B partitions and rollback.

The face, voice and phone page are a rewrite, not a port: DeskSprite (Arduino, on the FNK0104N's 3.5" screen) is the reference for the face's look, expressions and behaviour and for the relay's protocol, but the code is new, sized for a 240 × 280 portrait screen and the P4's ESP-IDF stack. It lives in this repository (§14).

9.4Phone controller and network

The board joins the home Wi-Fi (voice needs the relay); the controller is at micropup.local. With no known network it falls back to its own access point, micropup-XXXX, with phone control and video but no voice.

  • / is the controller: the camera view behind a joystick for forward and turning, action and expression buttons, a tail control, a talk button and an E-stop. It reads the browser's Gamepad API.
  • /status, /settings and /ota for telemetry, configuration and updates.
  • WebSocket on /ws, compact JSON as in the earlier design: cmd (vx, wz) at 20–30 Hz, act (stand, lie_down, estop, face, tail) on events, tel at 10 Hz, nak with the reason for a refused action.
  • Dead-man: no cmd for 300 ms sends zero velocity; no connection for 5 s lies the robot down (S2).

9.5Face, voice, sound, petting and tail

Face. DeskSprite's look on the small portrait screen: cyan rounded eyes on black, blinks and glances, and its expressions (listening, thinking, speaking, idle, happy, sad, surprise, love, sleepy, warning), plus the robot's own: dizzy and help when fallen, confused when the phone is lost. The state mapping is the earlier design's: idle when lying or standing still, happy while walking, surprise on a bump, love when petted, sleepy on low battery, warning with a FAULT strip on a fault or E-stop (never read as a mood).

Stabilized eyes and gaze. The eyes shift opposite to the body's roll and pitch, clamped to a few pixels, and look toward the face the camera finds.

Voice. DeskSprite's relay protocol, reimplemented on the P4: push-to-talk from the button on the back or the phone. The robot talks but voice does not move it in v1; v1.1 maps phrases to actions under the same limits. Whether the kit has a usable microphone is checked in V0 (§13).

Sound. Short clips through the board's codec and the kit's speaker: a yip on starting to walk, a startled one on a bump, a whine for help, a sigh on low battery. Rate-limited, with volume and mute.

Petting. The screen has no touch, so a tap on the head is read by the IMU's tap detection: love and a tail wag. Taps while walking are ignored.

Button on the back. A short press talks; a long press is an E-stop (S1).

Tail. Core 0 picks the pose or wag; core 1 drives it, limits it while walking and switches it off in E-stop.

9.6Mode state machine

Stand only when upright: belly-down or standing within 20° of level by the gravity vector; anything else is refused with a reason (S10). Without servo feedback, STAND_UP starts by commanding the lying pose slowly, since the firmware cannot read where the legs actually are (unless the pot read-back is fitted).

9.7Safety requirements

All of them are enforced on core 1.

IDRequirement
S1E-stop from the phone or a long press of the back button switches the servo rail off (the power switch) within one control cycle and latches until cleared.
S2Dead-man: 300 ms without a valid command sets velocity to zero; 5 s without the phone lies the robot down.
S3Fall detection per §9.6, going limp to protect the gears and the screen.
S4Servo protection without servo feedback: the servo rail's current from the INA226 (sustained high current means servos straining or stalled: lie down and rest); a rest timer that lies the robot down after a set walking time, tuned in H5 from servo case temperatures measured by hand or thermocouple; a stall check on the gap between command and the servo's reported angle.
S5Low battery: warn, then lie down and switch the servos off before the pack is over-discharged; floor about 6.6 V, smoothed over about 10 s.
S6Commands are clamped to the policy's training ranges, whatever core 0 sends.
S7A policy whose contract hash does not match the build cannot enable the servos.
S8S1–S7 and S9–S12 are covered by automated tests on the host twin where they can be.
S9Hang protection: a task watchdog on control_task and imu_task resets the board; the servo power switch's pull-down keeps the rail off from reset until core 1 enables it, so the servos go limp. The power switch stays reachable.
S10Stand only when upright (belly-down or standing, within 20° of level). The refusal and its reason reach the face and the phone.
S11Obstacle stop: with an obstacle closer than about 15 cm in the chin sensor's forward zones, forward velocity is clamped to zero; backing away and turning stay allowed.
S12Edge stop: when the chin sensor's lower zones stop seeing the floor ahead (a table edge or the top of a stair), forward velocity is clamped to zero and the robot backs off a step.

9.8Vision in v1

  • Camera view: an MJPEG stream (QVGA to VGA, 10–15 fps) from the P4's hardware encoder, behind the joystick on the controller page.
  • Eye contact: a small face finder (esp-dl's human-face detector or similar, a few frames per second) moves the on-screen eyes toward the largest face.
  • Nothing from vision moves the legs in v1.

11v2 autonomy

  • Following people, detected on the robot. Espressif's pedestrian detector (esp-dl, 224 × 224) runs at about 50 ms a frame on an ESP32-P4 by Espressif's own table; sharing the chip with streaming and the face, expect 5–15 fps, measured in V0. Its target's bearing and size become velocity commands, under core 1's limits and the obstacle and edge stops.
  • Patrolling. Wandering with the chin sensor's 8 × 8 zones for avoidance (it can tell which side is clear), printed markers on doorways and a dock.
  • Conversation with sight. "What do you see?" sends a frame through the relay to a vision-capable model.
  • Off-board vision, optional. Heavier models (recognising family members or objects) can run in a container on the VPS (trinsicstudios-1: 6 vCPUs, 8 GB, no GPU), or on Neil's Raspberry Pi 4 at home, reached through the VPS over Tailscale when the robot is away, as described in the archived PRD (v0.30, §11).

13Open questions

  1. The bus servo's size, mounting tabs, spline, weight, gear material, speed and position resolution (H0, on arrival), and how far the braced thigh's servo pocket has to change.
  2. How the real bus servo compares with the MG92B model the simulation uses (H0), and how consistent the four are.
  3. Servo power on the robot: the Waveshare bus adapter needs 9–12.6 V, more than a 2S pack (7.4–8.4 V), and its 6 V buck adapter gives 3 A peak, about three servos at stall. Options: a 3S pack with a 6 V buck per leg, or a direct half-duplex connection from the board with a 2S pack and a larger 6 V regulator. Decided before H3; the bench uses a 12 V wall adapter.
  4. How much a looser later walk should use abduction; the first walk keeps it within ±6° (b6, §7.2).
  5. Cost: twelve servos put the BOM at about $340–390 (G2), close to a Microduck's $399. Accepted (11 November 2026).
  6. Does the Waveshare board fit in the 46 mm-wide body (the CAD has a 60 × 40 mm stand-in), or does the robot need the smaller FireBeetle 2 (no audio codec, a PDM microphone only)?
  7. Does the Waveshare kit have a usable microphone for voice, or does one need adding (an I2S MEMS microphone, about $5)?
  8. The camera for the robot: the kit's RPi Camera (B) if its field of view is enough, otherwise a wide-angle module ESP-IDF's MIPI driver supports (OV5647 class).
  9. Touch: the 1.69" screen's touch version, or IMU taps only.
  10. IMU choice: LSM6DSOX (tap detection on chip) or another breakout.
  11. Battery capacity and runtime: 450 mAh against twelve servos and the mass budget.
  12. PETG or PLA for the braced leg parts (fit coupons and a test leg first).
  13. The face's design at 240 × 280 portrait, and which DeskSprite expressions carry over.
  14. Pollen's action low-pass (their runtime filters the targets, their training does not): settle in the contract before H5.

15Notes for Claude Code

  • The 12-servo braced MicroPup in this document is the target. The XL330 design and the biped are archived; don't revive them unless Neil asks.
  • Cost is a requirement (G2). With twelve servos the BOM is about $340–390; flag anything that pushes it further. Buy from Amazon and Adafruit where they stock the part (the servos come from MangDang). Neil prefers parts that need no soldering.
  • Work milestone by milestone (§10).
  • Code in firmware/micropup/components/core never includes ESP-IDF headers; it reaches hardware only through the HAL, and every core change must build and pass tests on the twin.
  • All safety lives on core 1. Core 0 may only ask; core 1 clamps and decides, and only core 1 touches the I2C bus and the servo outputs.
  • The face and voice are new code. DeskSprite is the reference for look and behaviour, not a code base to copy wholesale.
  • Never hand-edit generated policy headers; regenerate them with tools/policy_export.
  • In the simulation: every process of a run uses the same QUAD_* environment variables (QUAD_SERVO=mg92b QUAD_DESIGN=micro QUAD_FEEDBACK=pot QUAD_LEG=braced, plus the study's switches); only *_collision geoms collide; actuators are declared in joint order; an N-iteration rsl_rl run ends with model_(N-1).pt.
  • The sim repository's origin is Pollen's; never push to it.
  • Hardware steps (wiring, soldering, opening servos, flashing, stand tests, weighing parts) are manual. Flag them for Neil rather than assuming results.
  • Record significant decisions as ADRs in docs/decisions/.
