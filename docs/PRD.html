1Summary

MicroPup is a small four-legged walking robot dog, about 16 cm long and about 320 g, built to cost well under a ready-made robot. Its face is a 1.69" screen (240 × 280, portrait) in a yellow bezel at the front of the body, with a camera on a strip above it and a distance sensor in the chin. Behind it is a rounded white body on four legs, two MG90S hobby servos per leg, with a paddle tail on a micro servo.

Walking comes from a reinforcement-learning policy trained in simulation with the tools of Pollen Robotics' Microduck (microduck_rl, mjlab), with a model of the hobby servo in place of Pollen's XL330 model, and runs on the robot at 50 Hz. MicroPup is driven from a phone browser, talks through DeskSprite's voice relay, and shows a first-person camera view. Following people and patrolling are v2 (§11).

Four decisions shape everything else:

  • Cost first. About $150–195 for everything, a third to a half of a $399 Microduck. The servos are about $25 of it. Anything that pushes the total toward the Microduck's price has to earn its place.
  • One board. A Waveshare ESP32-P4 board runs the walking loop, the safety checks, the face, voice, camera and Wi-Fi. The walking loop and safety get one core to themselves (§9.2).
  • Hobby servos, measured. The MG90S has no feedback and no published model. It is modelled from its datasheet for now and measured on the bench before the robot is trusted (H0, §10).
  • Simulation first, as before: the body, the walking policy and the firmware's logic are proven in simulation before the robot walks.

MicroPup replaced the MicroCluck biped on 9 November 2026 and was redesigned for cost on 10 November 2026. The repository keeps the MicroCluck name. It is a personal project and is not affiliated with Pollen Robotics, Hugging Face, Stanford or Waveshare.

2Goals and non-goals

v1 goals

IDGoal
G1Stand and walk on flat indoor floors from velocity commands: forward and backward, turning, and both at once. The speed targets are set from the simulation (§7.2); at least 0.15 m/s forward is the bar. No sideways steps.
G2A bill of materials of about $150–195 for everything, including spares and a battery charger.
G3Phone control from any browser (iOS and Android), no app install, with a live camera view.
G4An expressive face in DeskSprite's style on the 1.69" screen, mapped to the robot's state, with a tail that adds to the expression (§9.5).
G5Safe failure: dead-man stop, fall detection, servo protection without servo feedback, low-battery handling, E-stop, an obstacle stop and a table-edge stop from the chin sensor.
G6OTA updates; live telemetry for sim2real debugging.
G7Personality: IMU-stabilized eyes, eyes that follow a face the camera sees, a wagging tail, sounds, and voice conversation through DeskSprite's relay.
G8Get up from lying on its belly, and lie down on command. The rest pose is lying down.
G9Prove the design, the walking policy and the firmware's logic in simulation before the robot walks.

v2 goals

  • Follow a person, with person detection on the P4 (§11).
  • Patrol: wander with obstacle avoidance using the chin sensor's 8 × 8 zones, recognise printed markers on doorways and a dock.
  • Voice commands that move it ("come here", "sit", "lie down").

Non-goals for v1

  • Autonomous navigation of any kind (v2).
  • Sideways walking, and getting up from the back or a side (two servos per leg cannot do either); a person rights a robot that has rolled over.
  • Rough terrain, stairs, carrying a payload.
  • Long walks: hobby servos and a small battery give short sessions with rests (§6.4, S4).
  • Any commercial use.

3Development approach

Phase A builds MicroPup in simulation: the model, the walking policy, the design checks, the C port of the policy and the virtual MicroPup (§8). Phase B builds the robot, starting with a bench measurement of the servo (H0) that replaces the datasheet servo model. Because the parts are cheap and in stock, bench bring-up can start as soon as they arrive, alongside Phase A.

4Reference designs and constraints

MicroPup takes the idea of a small learned-gait dog from Stanford's Pupper V3, the training stack from Pollen's Microduck, and the face, voice and personality from Neil's DeskSprite. Small commercial robot dogs of about this size and weight walk on hobby-class servos, which is the evidence that the servo class can do it.

  • Pollen's microduck_rl is Apache-2.0. MicroPup uses its training recipe and tasks in a fork. None of Microduck's meshes or mechanical design is used.

MG90S key specs

About 13.4 g, 22.8 × 12.2 × 28.5 mm, metal gears. Stall torque about 1.8 kg·cm at 4.8 V and 2.2 kg·cm (0.216 N·m) at 6 V; about 0.10 s/60° at 4.8 V. Input 4.8–6.0 V. Position command by pulse width, about 1000–2000 µs over about 180°, at a 50 Hz frame. No position, speed, load or temperature feedback; the potentiometer inside can be wired out for a position reading (§6.3). Clones vary widely in torque, deadband and centring, so buy from one reputable seller and measure them (H0).

5System architecture

  • Core 1 owns everything that moves: the 50 Hz policy loop, the PCA9685, the IMU, the chin sensor, the power monitor and the state machine. All safety checks live here (§9.7).
  • Core 0 runs everything else: the face on the screen, voice through the relay, the phone page and WebSocket, the camera stream and face finder, and the behaviour layer that turns phone, voice and (in v2) autonomy into velocity commands. It can only ask: commands pass to core 1 through a queue, and core 1 clamps them.
  • Why one board. The P4 is fast enough for all of it, and one board removes the second and third boards, the link between them and their wiring, which was much of the old design's cost and complexity. The price is that a crash anywhere resets everything; the servo outputs are wired to go limp on any reset (S9).

6.3Servo drive

  • PCA9685 at 50 Hz, one frame per control cycle, written in one I2C burst at 400 kHz or 1 MHz (well under 1 ms). Its 12-bit counter gives about 4.9 µs steps, about 0.45° on an MG90S.
  • Channels: 0–7 the leg servos in joint order (FL hip, FL knee, FR hip, FR knee, RL hip, RL knee, RR hip, RR knee), 8 the tail. The pulse width for each joint's zero and its direction are calibrated per servo in H0 and stored on the board.
  • Limp on reset: OE is pulled high, so from power-on and after any reset the outputs are off and the servos go limp until core 1 enables them (S9). Some digital servos hold their last position without pulses; the MG90S is checked for this in H0.
  • No read-back: the servos stay sealed and stock. The scripted gait runs open loop (§7.2); the robot knows its legs only from what it last commanded, so every motion starts from a known pose (§9.6). Only the H0 bench servos have their potentiometer wired out, to measure them.

6.4Power

  • Servo rail: 6 V from a 5 A regulator off the 2S pack. Eight servos near stall could draw several amps; measure walking current in H1 and size the fuse from it. At least 470 µF of bulk capacitance at the PCA9685's V+.
  • Logic rail: a 5 V, 2 A buck into the board. The P4 streaming video with Wi-Fi peaks at about 1.3 A.
  • Monitoring: the INA226 measures the pack voltage and the servo rail's current (S4, S5).
  • Runtime: a 450 mAh pack at an assumed 1–2 A average while walking gives roughly 15–25 minutes of walking, longer idling. Measure it in H5; a bigger pack fits if the mass budget allows (§13).
  • Charging: off the robot with a balance charger. A charging dock is v2.

6.5Mechanical

The simulation model (build_quad_model.py with QUAD_SERVO=mg90s QUAD_DESIGN=micro, §7.1) is the current design. OpenSCAD in hardware/ takes its numbers from it in H3.

  • Body: a rounded box 160 mm long and 70 mm wide, the top sloping toward the tail. Hip servos inside, shafts out through the side walls, hips 110 mm apart front to rear. The battery lies in the belly; the board sits on a layer above, slid fore and aft to set the balance.
  • Face: the 1.69" module (about 33 × 40 mm) in portrait at the front, leaning back 10°, in a yellow bezel with soft lips proud of the glass.
  • Camera on the forehead: on a strip of the bezel above the screen, tilted about 25° up, so it sees people's faces rather than their feet; a wide lens if the kit's camera is too narrow (§13). The camera ribbon runs back to the board.
  • VL53L5CX in the chin: looking ahead and slightly down, so its upper zones see obstacles and its lower zones see the floor ahead (S11, S12). A plain opening or ST's recommended cover glass, to avoid reflections.
  • Legs: thigh 45 mm and shank 45 mm, axis to axis and knee to foot; standing height 75 mm from the hip axis to the foot centre (82 mm trunk height). Printed PETG links (aluminium bar at 3/8" × 3/32" if printed links flex), TPU ball feet 14 mm across. The knee servo rides on the thigh.
  • Tail: the paddle tail of the earlier design, scaled down, on an SG90 at the rear of the body. Expression only.
  • IMU: rigidly mounted near the hip midpoint; its pose goes into the model.

7Simulation and training

Every run's numbers are in the study, quadruped-study.html. This section keeps what the rest of the document depends on.

7.1Model and task

  1. Fork. pollen-robotics/microduck_rl is forked; the dog lives on the branch microcluck_quad in the worktree ~/microcluck_quad, not pushed. It becomes the sim/ submodule.
  2. Model. src/mjlab_microduck/robot/quad/build_quad_model.py. QUAD_SERVO=mg90s picks the servo and scales the body around it; QUAD_DESIGN=micro adds the 1.69" face, the one-board electronics and the small battery (files robot_quad8_mg90s_micro*.xml). Every process of a run (training, evaluation, video) must see the same environment variables. The first micro runs have the camera in the chin; it moves to the forehead in the next model (a few grams, not expected to change the results).
  3. Servo model. HobbyServoActuator (src/mjlab_microduck/actuator/servo_models.py, parameters in robot/quad/params/mg90s_m1.json): a DC-motor model from the datasheet (stall 0.216 N·m at 6 V, no-load about 13 rad/s), a ±0.5° deadband and a proportional position loop. It is an estimate, replaced by a fitted model after H0. Supply 5.6–6.0 V with sag, command delay 2–5 steps.
  4. Feedback. QUAD_FEEDBACK=pot gives the policy potentiometer-grade joint angles (±0.02 rad noise, ±0.05 rad bias per joint, no joint velocities); blind gives it no joint feedback at all.
  5. Collision naming and actuator order as before: only *_collision geoms collide in the GPU simulator, and actuators are declared in joint order.
  6. Task. Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroCluck-Quad8, Pollen's velocity recipe adapted for the dog: no sideways tracking, a bucket of backward commands, tighter tracking at slow speeds, a staged torque penalty, Pollen's randomization plus trunk mass 0.95–1.15 and centre of mass ±15 mm.
  7. Observation contract v2: gyro (3), projected gravity (3), joint positions (8, pot only), last action (8), twist command (3): 25 values with pot read-back, 17 blind. Actions are 8 joint targets. The tail is not in the policy.

Background. On 9 November an MG90S dog built around the old design (414 g, with the 60 g FNK0104N face board) stood and got up from its belly every time but barely walked (at most 0.06 m/s), with every joint at its stall torque. The micro design takes about 100 g out of the body to give the servos that margin back. A Feetech STS3215 dog was also tried and rejected (study §8).

What the result means. The servos can walk this body; training just did not find how. Standing (0.02 N·m) and belly stand-up (about 0.1 N·m) are well inside the servo, and a hand-written trot (hip ±0.20 rad, knee lift 0.40 rad, diagonal pairs half a cycle apart, 2.5 Hz) walks at 0.16 m/s, steers by shortening the stride on one side, and survives the play pushes. v1 therefore walks with a scripted gait: the walk cycle is played as joint targets, the IMU is used for falls and later for balance corrections, and no neural network runs on the body. The MG90S model is still a datasheet estimate, so H0 (bench measurement) stays first. A stronger servo, the TowerPro MG92B (0.343 N·m stall, same speed, 13.8 g, about $8–12), was simulated next; see below.

7.3Policy export pipeline

Lives in tools/policy_export/, unchanged from the earlier design: ONNX with the normalizer baked in, folded into the first layer, emitted as a C header with metadata and the contract hash, golden vectors checked on the board (maximum error ≤ 1e-4), and a closed-loop host test driving MuJoCo.

7.4Sim2real contract

docs/sim2real-contract.md is the single, versioned, hashed source of truth for the firmware and the simulation: observation order, units and scaling (§7.1); where joint angles come from (pot read-back or none); action post-processing and how a joint target becomes a pulse width (per-servo zero, direction and scale from H0); command ranges; loop rate and expected delay. The firmware refuses to enable the servos if the policy's contract hash does not match its build (S7).

7.5Getting up

  • From the belly: a scripted push-up from lying belly-down with the legs along the sides, as in the earlier design. It is the normal way to stand; the robot rests lying down.
  • From the back or a side: not possible with two servos per leg. The robot detects it (S3), goes limp, shows a help face and asks for help.

8Virtual MicroPup

The virtual MicroPup runs the real firmware's control core against the simulator on the PC, so the phone page or a test script drives the simulated dog exactly as it would the robot. It is the regression harness for every safety requirement.

Hardware abstraction layer. The control core (control loop, state machine, safety, policy inference, command handling) is plain C with no ESP-IDF includes. It reaches the world through: servo outputs (pulse widths, enable and disable), joint angles (pot read-back, if fitted), IMU (gyro and gravity), clock (50 Hz tick, sim time on the host), range (the chin sensor's zones), power (pack voltage and servo current) and the command queue. The host twin runs MuJoCo with the same servo model as training, quantizes the pulse widths like the PCA9685, models command delay, IMU noise and ToF ray casts, and can inject low battery, pushes, obstacles, table edges and a stalled control task. It writes the robot's log format and runs headless for automated tests.

9Firmware

9.1One firmware, rewritten

ESP-IDF v5.x in C, FreeRTOS, on the ESP32-P4 (the P4's camera, hardware JPEG and AI functions need ESP-IDF). LVGL for the face, Espressif's MIPI camera stack and hardware JPEG encoder for the stream, esp-dl's face and pedestrian detectors, Wi-Fi through esp_hosted on the ESP32-C6. Updates over the air with A/B partitions and rollback.

The face, voice and phone page are a rewrite, not a port: DeskSprite (Arduino, on the FNK0104N's 3.5" screen) is the reference for the face's look, expressions and behaviour and for the relay's protocol, but the code is new, sized for a 240 × 280 portrait screen and the P4's ESP-IDF stack. It lives in this repository (§14).

9.4Phone controller and network

The board joins the home Wi-Fi (voice needs the relay); the controller is at micropup.local. With no known network it falls back to its own access point, micropup-XXXX, with phone control and video but no voice.

  • / is the controller: the camera view behind a joystick for forward and turning, action and expression buttons, a tail control, a talk button and an E-stop. It reads the browser's Gamepad API.
  • /status, /settings and /ota for telemetry, configuration and updates.
  • WebSocket on /ws, compact JSON as in the earlier design: cmd (vx, wz) at 20–30 Hz, act (stand, lie_down, estop, face, tail) on events, tel at 10 Hz, nak with the reason for a refused action.
  • Dead-man: no cmd for 300 ms sends zero velocity; no connection for 5 s lies the robot down (S2).

9.5Face, voice, sound, petting and tail

Face. DeskSprite's look on the small portrait screen: cyan rounded eyes on black, blinks and glances, and its expressions (listening, thinking, speaking, idle, happy, sad, surprise, love, sleepy, warning), plus the robot's own: dizzy and help when fallen, confused when the phone is lost. The state mapping is the earlier design's: idle when lying or standing still, happy while walking, surprise on a bump, love when petted, sleepy on low battery, warning with a FAULT strip on a fault or E-stop (never read as a mood).

Stabilized eyes and gaze. The eyes shift opposite to the body's roll and pitch, clamped to a few pixels, and look toward the face the camera finds.

Voice. DeskSprite's relay protocol, reimplemented on the P4: push-to-talk from the button on the back or the phone. The robot talks but voice does not move it in v1; v1.1 maps phrases to actions under the same limits. Whether the kit has a usable microphone is checked in V0 (§13).

Sound. Short clips through the board's codec and the kit's speaker: a yip on starting to walk, a startled one on a bump, a whine for help, a sigh on low battery. Rate-limited, with volume and mute.

Petting. The screen has no touch, so a tap on the head is read by the IMU's tap detection: love and a tail wag. Taps while walking are ignored.

Button on the back. A short press talks; a long press is an E-stop (S1).

Tail. Core 0 picks the pose or wag; core 1 drives it, limits it while walking and switches it off in E-stop.

9.6Mode state machine

Stand only when upright: belly-down or standing within 20° of level by the gravity vector; anything else is refused with a reason (S10). Without servo feedback, STAND_UP starts by commanding the lying pose slowly, since the firmware cannot read where the legs actually are (unless the pot read-back is fitted).

9.7Safety requirements

All of them are enforced on core 1.

IDRequirement
S1E-stop from the phone or a long press of the back button switches the servo outputs off (PCA9685 OE high) within one control cycle and latches until cleared.
S2Dead-man: 300 ms without a valid command sets velocity to zero; 5 s without the phone lies the robot down.
S3Fall detection per §9.6, going limp to protect the gears and the screen.
S4Servo protection without servo feedback: the servo rail's current from the INA226 (sustained high current means servos straining or stalled: lie down and rest); a rest timer that lies the robot down after a set walking time, tuned in H5 from servo case temperatures measured by hand or thermocouple; with pot read-back, a stall check on the gap between command and angle.
S5Low battery: warn, then lie down and switch the servos off before the pack is over-discharged; floor about 6.6 V, smoothed over about 10 s.
S6Commands are clamped to the policy's training ranges, whatever core 0 sends.
S7A policy whose contract hash does not match the build cannot enable the servos.
S8S1–S7 and S9–S12 are covered by automated tests on the host twin where they can be.
S9Hang protection: a task watchdog on control_task and imu_task resets the board; OE's pull-up keeps the outputs off from reset until core 1 enables them, so the servos go limp. The power switch stays reachable.
S10Stand only when upright (belly-down or standing, within 20° of level). The refusal and its reason reach the face and the phone.
S11Obstacle stop: with an obstacle closer than about 15 cm in the chin sensor's forward zones, forward velocity is clamped to zero; backing away and turning stay allowed.
S12Edge stop: when the chin sensor's lower zones stop seeing the floor ahead (a table edge or the top of a stair), forward velocity is clamped to zero and the robot backs off a step.

9.8Vision in v1

  • Camera view: an MJPEG stream (QVGA to VGA, 10–15 fps) from the P4's hardware encoder, behind the joystick on the controller page.
  • Eye contact: a small face finder (esp-dl's human-face detector or similar, a few frames per second) moves the on-screen eyes toward the largest face.
  • Nothing from vision moves the legs in v1.

11v2 autonomy

  • Following people, detected on the robot. Espressif's pedestrian detector (esp-dl, 224 × 224) runs at about 50 ms a frame on an ESP32-P4 by Espressif's own table; sharing the chip with streaming and the face, expect 5–15 fps, measured in V0. Its target's bearing and size become velocity commands, under core 1's limits and the obstacle and edge stops.
  • Patrolling. Wandering with the chin sensor's 8 × 8 zones for avoidance (it can tell which side is clear), printed markers on doorways and a dock.
  • Conversation with sight. "What do you see?" sends a frame through the relay to a vision-capable model.
  • Off-board vision, optional. Heavier models (recognising family members or objects) can run in a container on the VPS (trinsicstudios-1: 6 vCPUs, 8 GB, no GPU), or on Neil's Raspberry Pi 4 at home, reached through the VPS over Tailscale when the robot is away, as described in the archived PRD (v0.30, §11).

12Risks and mitigations

RiskMitigation
The policy does not walk well enough on hobby servosSimulation first (§7.2); a lighter body; pot read-back; a scripted gait as the fallback (§7.2).
The servo model is wrong (datasheet estimate)Measure the servos in H0 and refit the model before trusting the policy (S6); randomize delay, supply and friction in training.
Servos vary, strip or overheatOne reputable seller; per-servo calibration (H0); two spares in the pack and more cheaply; rail-current overload check and a rest timer (S4); short sessions.
No feedback: the robot does not know where its legs areSlow, scripted starts from a known pose; the IMU for falls; pot read-back if the simulation or H0 says it is needed.

A crash anywhere stops everythingWatchdog reset; servo outputs off from reset (S9); the robot lies limp rather than thrashing. Core 0 code kept out of the control path.

The Waveshare board does not fit the 70 mm bodyChecked against the board's real outline in H3; the DFRobot FireBeetle 2 ESP32-P4 (25.4 × 60 mm) as the smaller alternative, which lacks the audio codec (§13).
Software rewrite is bigger than expectedFace, voice and phone page come after the walking robot; a plain face and the phone page are enough for H5.
Short runtimeTwo packs; measure in H5; a larger pack if the mass budget allows.

13Open questions

  1. MG90S or MG92B for the legs, and with it the pot read-back? MG90S: scripted gait only, sealed servos, cheapest. MG92B with the pot read-back: the scripted gait plus a learned walk that tracks 0.1–0.3 m/s in simulation, for about $40–60 more and the ADC wiring (§7.2). The legs will also gain bracket and bearing weight the model does not have yet, which favours the MG92B.
  2. How the real MG90S compares with the datasheet model (H0), and which seller's servos are consistent.
  3. Does the Waveshare board fit in a 70 mm-wide body, or does the robot need the smaller FireBeetle 2 (no audio codec, a PDM microphone only)?
  4. Does the Waveshare kit have a usable microphone for voice, or does one need adding (an I2S MEMS microphone, about $5)?
  5. The camera for the robot: the kit's RPi Camera (B) if its field of view is enough, otherwise a wide-angle module ESP-IDF's MIPI driver supports (OV5647 class).
  6. Touch: the 1.69" screen's touch version, or IMU taps only.
  7. IMU choice: LSM6DSOX (tap detection on chip) or another breakout.
  8. Battery capacity and runtime: 450 mAh against the mass budget.
  9. Printed PETG legs or thin aluminium bar.
  10. The face's design at 240 × 280 portrait, and which DeskSprite expressions carry over.
  11. Pollen's action low-pass (their runtime filters the targets, their training does not): settle in the contract before H5.

15Notes for Claude Code

  • The micro MicroPup in this document is the target. The XL330 design and the biped are archived; don't revive them unless Neil asks.
  • Cost is a requirement (G2). Flag anything that pushes the BOM toward $200 or more.
  • Work milestone by milestone (§10).
  • Code in firmware/micropup/components/core never includes ESP-IDF headers; it reaches hardware only through the HAL, and every core change must build and pass tests on the twin.
  • All safety lives on core 1. Core 0 may only ask; core 1 clamps and decides, and only core 1 touches the I2C bus and the servo outputs.
  • The face and voice are new code. DeskSprite is the reference for look and behaviour, not a code base to copy wholesale.
  • Never hand-edit generated policy headers; regenerate them with tools/policy_export.
  • In the simulation: every process of a run uses the same QUAD_* environment variables (QUAD_SERVO=mg90s QUAD_DESIGN=micro QUAD_FEEDBACK=…); only *_collision geoms collide; actuators are declared in joint order; an N-iteration rsl_rl run ends with model_(N-1).pt.
  • The sim repository's origin is Pollen's; never push to it.
  • Hardware steps (wiring, soldering, opening servos, flashing, stand tests, weighing parts) are manual. Flag them for Neil rather than assuming results.
  • Record significant decisions as ADRs in docs/decisions/.
