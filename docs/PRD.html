1Summary

MicroCluck is a ~25 cm bipedal walking robot chicken in the style of Pollen Robotics' Microduck. Its whole torso is a vertical, front-facing Freenove ESP32-S3 3.5" touchscreen (FNK0104 family) showing an animated chicken face, with a comb tuft on top, flat wings on the sides, tail feathers at the back and clawed three-toed feet. It has no separate head, neck or beak.

Walking comes from a reinforcement-learning policy trained in simulation (forked from Pollen's open-source microduck_rl) and run entirely on the ESP32-S3 at 50 Hz. It is driven from a phone browser over the robot's own Wi-Fi. A claw on top is planned for v2.

Two decisions shape everything else:

  • Single MCU. The ESP32-S3 runs the control loop, policy inference, servo bus, IMU fusion, face, sound, Wi-Fi controller and OTA. No Raspberry Pi.
  • Simulation first. The robot is designed, trained and driven end to end in simulation, including the real firmware logic and face, before most parts are ordered (§3).

The name is a homage to Microduck. MicroCluck is a personal project and is not affiliated with Pollen Robotics or Hugging Face.

3Development approach

Work runs in two phases. Phase A builds MicroCluck in simulation: the model, the walking policy, a design validation report, the C port of the policy and a virtual MicroCluck that runs the real firmware logic and face against the simulator. Phase B builds the robot.

Bench bring-up (H0) needs only the Freenove board, which is already owned, so it runs alongside Phase A. Servos, battery and power parts are ordered at the design freeze after S3, once the simulator has settled stance width, mass and torque margins. Two kinds of hardware work may start earlier: driver bring-up on one or two early servos and an IMU (the start of H1 and H2), and fit-check prints of the board mount and bezel on the board you already own (the start of H3). The full exit criteria of H1 to H3 still wait for the freeze.

Facts carried over from Pollen's repos

  • Policies are trained at 50 Hz with PPO and exported to ONNX. The exporter bakes the observation normalizer into the ONNX graph.
  • The actor sees 61 values: 48 proprioception plus commands for twist (3), head pose (4) and body pose (6).
  • Actuator fidelity is the dominant sim2real gap at this scale. The XL330 is modeled down to its voltage control law, with randomized battery voltage, voltage sag, command delay and friction. Each task has a Backlash variant with ±1° of gear play.
  • Training needs a CUDA GPU; Pollen quotes 1–2 hours for a usable gait at 4096 parallel environments.
  • Robot models are exported from Onshape with onshape-to-robot. The CAD is not published, so MicroCluck edits the exported XML by hand (§7.1).
  • A simulated duck can run the real robot software against a body in MuJoCo, which is the model for the virtual MicroCluck (§8).
  • Licensing: code is Apache-2.0; STL meshes are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (non-commercial). The CAD is not published, and Pollen asks that the mechanical design not be called open-source hardware.

XL330-M288-T key specs

18 g. Stall torque 0.52 N·m at 5 V (1.47 A) and 0.60 N·m at 6 V (1.74 A); the Current Limit register defaults to 1.75 A, so about 0.60 N·m is the ceiling at pack voltage. Input 3.7–6.0 V, 5.0 V recommended; Microduck and MicroCluck run it from a 2S pack anyway (§6.4). TTL half-duplex bus with 3.3 V logic (5 V tolerant), 9.6 kbps to 4 Mbps, Protocol 2.0. Feedback includes position, velocity, current, temperature and input voltage. 12-bit absolute encoder. The estimated continuous torque is only ~0.10 N·m, 20% of stall, so thermal margin is a design constraint (§7.2, §9.8).

6.3Servo bus interface

  • The gate drives the bus only while DIR is high, then releases the line so servos can reply.
  • RX also hears the ESP32's own transmissions. The dxl driver discards that echo, or the UART's RS-485 mode is configured to ignore RX during TX.
  • 3.3 V logic matches the XL330's TTL level, so no level shifting is needed. Unused gates: inputs and enables tied to GND.
  • Built on perfboard with DIP parts and a socket for the chip. There is no surface-mount soldering anywhere in the build.

6.4Power

  • Servo rail: the 2S pack directly, from about 6.6 V near empty to 8.4 V fully charged and unloaded, with no regulator. This is what Microduck does and what the training model assumes: the stock simulation randomizes the servo supply from 6.5 to 8.2 V with load-dependent sag. It is above ROBOTIS's rated 3.7–6.0 V (5.0 V recommended), so it is an accepted risk (§12).
  • Servo settings for the higher voltage: the XL330's Max Voltage Limit register tops out at 7.0 V, so a fresh pack sets the Input Voltage Error bit and the Alert flag on status packets. Following Microduck, clear that bit in the Shutdown register (52 instead of the default 53) so torque is not cut, and have the dxl driver ignore that bit and the Alert flag when it checks health (S4).
  • Peak current: the Current Limit register caps each servo at 1.75 A by default, so the theoretical worst case with every servo stalled is about 17 A. Walking draws far less. Measure it in H1, then size the fuse and the BMS peak rating from the measurement, and put at least 1000 µF of bulk capacitance at the servo bus entry.
  • Logic rail: a separate 5 V buck from the pack into the Freenove board, with grounds joined at one star point. It must accept the pack's full range and ride through the sag that servo current causes.
  • Monitoring: the servos' Present Input Voltage register is the battery gauge, as on Microduck, averaged over about 10 s, with about 6.6 V as the empty floor (S5). The INA226 on the pack adds current.
  • Charging: the charger and BMS must hold the pack at or below 8.4 V.

6.5Mechanical

CAD starts in S1, because the model's masses and inertias come from it, and is finished in H3 once S3 has settled the stance width.

  • Legs: reuse Microduck's leg geometry from the STLs in microduck_rl (non-commercial license) to keep kinematic parity with Pollen's model. Only the torso and feet change, except that the hip position is open: with the stock layout the robot cannot get up from lying on its screen (§7.5).
  • Torso: screen frame with a raised bezel and rubber corner bumpers, glass recessed 2–3 mm. Forward falls land on the frame, not the glass: the frame curves outward at the top and bottom into soft (TPU) lips that stand proud of the glass. How far they must stand out comes from a drop test, not from the simulation. The bottom lip sits in front of the hip servos, where the thighs swing forward, so its clearance to the legs is checked the way the shell's is. Bezel in yellow, per the renders.
  • Comb: a tuft on top, snap-on on a top mounting boss. In the target look it is larger and feathered, so it is soft and light: it sits high, and its mass is part of the 15–30 g for comb and tail. The v2 claw uses the same boss and replaces the comb, or the comb becomes the claw's cover.
  • Wings: flat wings on both sides of the body, in a second colour. The shell prototype has 30 mm round discs as stand-ins for feather-shaped plates (3.5 mm thick, about 4 g each if printed solid, less hollow). They sit high on the body, 26 mm or more clear of the legs in every checked pose, and are decorative. They are separate prints, so a different outline changes only the wing file and the wing clearance check.
  • Body shell: a hollow shell runs back from the screen bezel's back face and holds the battery and electronics, with an opening in the rear wall for the tail's mounting stub, a USB-C slot, a power-switch hole and vents. It is a parametric OpenSCAD part in hardware/ (torso.scad for the layout, shell.scad for the body). The default is the target-look egg: 70 mm deep, with the top flush with the bezel and sloping about 20 degrees, a rounded rear edge, an underside that rises 18 mm toward the rear, and the tail behind the rear wall. Three earlier shapes are kept for comparison: a straight-sided box and a compact egg, both 52 mm deep, and a deep egg, 86 mm deep, which wraps the placeholder tail block. All four share the bezel's 66 mm width and 100 mm height. The simulation checks that the battery and electronics (and the tail block, where a shell wraps it) fit in the exported cavity, measures the thinnest wall, and measures how close the legs and wing discs come to the shell over MicroCluck's own gait (all 96,000 recorded poses) plus a joint-range stress sweep. The table below compares the four. Fastening to the bezel and tail, and the battery door, are not designed yet.
  • Tail: feathers in slightly flexible TPU, so the tail doubles as a bumper for backward falls and a third contact point when sitting. It shifts the center of mass backward, so its mass and collision shape go into the model. The tail is a separate feather piece at the back, attached to the shell's rear wall, so the shell ends where the tail begins and does not wrap it. The tail placeholder in the model sits behind that wall, at x = −63 mm (it was −45 mm), with the same mass and size. If the tail is only a small tuft it cannot be the sitting contact point, so the sit design (§9.7) needs either a larger tail or a rear pad on the shell.
  • Feet: three-toed chicken feet on a flat sole, which is the designed contact surface. The feet keep Pollen's foot and ankle interface. The claws on the front of the sole are mostly decorative in the target look: short and soft, and not counted as contact, so the plain sole's contact patch and the trained policy stay as they are. An optional second design fuses three chunky, floor-touching toes onto the front wall of the sole. They are a parametric OpenSCAD part in hardware/: one parameter table (params.scad) drives the printed toes, their appearance in the simulation and the simulation's collision shape, the convex hull of Pollen's sole plus the toes. They extend the contact patch 17 mm forward and change the walking dynamics, so the plain sole stays the default, and the plain-versus-toes training comparison (S2, S3) is only needed if the toes are kept. If they are kept, shorten the inner toe or leave the toes in the collision model, so the policy learns to avoid foot-to-foot contact on narrow steps.
  • Stance: hip spacing is decided in simulation (§7.2). Wider spacing stands steadier but makes each step's side-to-side weight shift larger.
  • IMU: rigidly mounted to the torso frame. Its exact pose relative to the model's base link goes into the model.
  • Cabling: daisy-chain routing with strain relief across the hip joints.

7Simulation and training

7.1Fork and adapt microduck_rl

  1. Fork pollen-robotics/microduck_rl and include it as the sim/ submodule. Reproduce Pollen's stock walking policy first (S0).
  2. Edit Pollen's exported XML by hand. Their onshape-to-robot configs (config_mjcf_*.json) cannot be re-run because the CAD is not published. Keep Pollen's leg meshes, and build the torso, comb and tail from primitive shapes whose masses and inertias come from the H3 CAD, or from estimates until it exists.
  3. Create MicroCluck versions of the walking model, robot_walk.xml, and its backlash variant (Pollen's add_backlash.py generates these). Velocity loads the walk models. The robot_groundcontact*.xml and robot_allcollisions*.xml models belong to the v1.1 tasks (StandUp and VelStand). Keep the two sole geoms named left_foot_collision and right_foot_collision, because the collision and contact setup finds them by name.
  4. Remove the neck and head joints (neck_pitch, head_pitch, head_yaw, head_roll) and the beak.
  5. Add the torso with measured or estimated mass, center of mass and inertia for the board, frame, battery, comb and tail. Give the tail a collision shape.
  6. Replace the feet with the MicroCluck sole (and toes, if kept long). Set the hip spacing.
  7. Re-derive the home pose and the standing trunk height from the new model, because Pollen's values assume the head's mass. A rest pose must hold for about 3 s from noisy starts, with tilt checked as well as height.
  8. Change the joint count from 14 to 10 everywhere: actuators, observations, actions, joint-index constants and the repo's regression tests, which lock in joint-index mappings. Keep the head-pose and body-pose command slots in the observation, padded with tiny sampling ranges (about ±0.001) so the inputs stay alive, because Pollen's rule is never to delete a slot. The firmware always sends zeros there. Remove the head-pose reward terms (head_pose_tracking, head_pose_bias) and their curriculum entries, which have no joints left to act on.
  9. Observation contract v1 (proposed, 49 values): gyro (3), projected gravity (3), joint positions (10), joint velocities (10), last action (10), then Pollen's 13-slot command block: twist (3), head pose (4, always zero) and body pose (6, always zero). This is Pollen's runtime layout with 10 joints in place of 14, as their robotd design doc describes it. Zero-padding keeps the fork's code and tests close to upstream. The 10 dead inputs cost about 2.5k parameters and can be pruned later.
  10. Actor network: Pollen's actor is 512-256-128 with ELU, about 190k parameters or 0.76 MB as float32 at 49 inputs. The first MicroCluck run keeps it, so the only change from the stock duck is the body. A smaller 256-128-64 actor (about 55k parameters or 218 KB) is an S2 ablation for the ESP32. The critic can be any size because it is never deployed.
  11. Domain randomization: keep Pollen's defaults, including the 6.5–8.2 V servo supply with load-dependent sag, which now matches the pack-powered robot, and add torso mass and center of mass ±10–15% for measurement error, plus a 0–100 g payload at the top of the torso so v1 walking survives the v2 claw.
  12. Train the Backlash variants. Reduce num-envs if the 3060's 12 GB runs out.

v1 tasks: Velocity-Flat, where standing is a zero command. v1.1: VelStand for walking plus fall recovery, and SitStand, which can use the tail as a support point. v1 still needs a sit for the dead-man, low-battery and over-temperature stops, but as a scripted joint trajectory (§9.7) rather than a policy, so it costs no training. S3 checks it (§7.2).

7.2Design validation (S3)

7.3Policy export pipeline

Lives in tools/policy_export/.

  1. Input is the ONNX file from Pollen's scripts/export.py, with the normalizer baked in. Never convert a checkpoint by hand.
  2. Extract weights, biases, activations and the normalizer. Fold the normalizer into the first layer: W' = W / σ and b' = b − W·(μ/σ).
  3. Emit policy_<name>.h with C arrays and metadata: input and output sizes, action scale, default pose and the contract hash.
  4. Generate golden vectors: at least 1000 plausible observations and their ONNX outputs.
  5. Device test: C inference against the golden vectors, maximum absolute error ≤ 1e-4.
  6. Closed-loop test: the same C inference, built for the host, drives MuJoCo. The gait must match the ONNX policy's.

7.4Sim2real contract

docs/sim2real-contract.md is the single, versioned, hashed source of truth. It must match Pollen's runtime (robotd) and constants (microduck_constants.py) exactly:

  • Observation order, units and scaling
  • Where joint velocity comes from: the servo register or a finite difference of position
  • Action post-processing: action scale, default pose offset, clipping
  • Servo control mode and firmware PD gains, since the sim emulates the servo's own PD loop
  • Command ranges, which the phone commands are clamped to
  • Loop rate (50 Hz) and expected command delay

Pollen's values, to confirm in S0. From their runtime docs: joint velocity comes from the servo's velocity register, read in the same fast sync read as position. Position P gain is 200 with I and D at zero, and 0.8 times that when standing. Training uses action scale 1.0, but their runtime runs 0.9 with the home pose as the offset, a deliberate deviation to understand before copying it. Return Delay Time is 0 and Shutdown is 52. Their runtime also applies a first-order low-pass to the leg targets, while the training repo says policies are trained unfiltered, so settle that in S0. Stock command ranges are ±0.4 m/s forward, ±0.3 m/s sideways and ±1.0 rad/s turning.

Firmware refuses to enable torque if a policy's contract hash doesn't match its build.

7.5Getting up and the hip position

Fall recovery is a v1.1 goal (§10, H7), and the plan so far assumed Pollen's leg layout could do it. In simulation it cannot, from lying on the screen, and the reason is geometry rather than training. The hips sit at the bottom end of the body (hip yaw axes at z = −5 mm, y = ±17.5 mm in the trunk frame), while the trunk's center of mass is 54 mm above the trunk frame, about 59 mm above the hip axes. Lying face down, the legs can only reach behind the weight, so pushing on the floor lifts the rear of the body and never brings the feet under it. Two learned stand-up runs from face-down starts ended with the policy lying still: the action noise shrank before any lift was found, also in a face-down-only run with three times the entropy bonus. An open-loop search (six keyframes of joint targets, legs mirrored, 5 s, 1,024 candidates over 40 generations) left the best tilt at 51°, and 0 of 64 random face-down starts stood up.

Raising the leg mounts. The same search on models with each leg chain moved up the body, as a bird's legs sit, level with the body. A start counts as up if the trunk is at standing height with the tilt under 20° for the last second (64 random face-down starts, random heading, no pushes). The simulator collides only geoms named for collision and switches every other geom off, and the stock stand-up model names only the soles and the torso pieces. In the first group of rows the thighs, shanks and hip brackets therefore pass through the floor and the body without penalty, which makes those results upper bounds. In the second group every leg part collides: the thigh and its two servos, the shank and its servo, and the hip bracket. The hip brackets may sit inside the shell, which assumes openings in it for them.

What is not shown.

  • Test-model leftovers.   • Packaging. Hips in the middle of the body do not fit the screen, battery and electronics as laid out.   • Pushes and speed. The raised-hip walker falls more under pushes and often stands still at 0.1 m/s. The open-loop search has no pushes, and nothing trains a getting-up policy that copes with them.

Decision. The hip position changes the torso, the shell, the battery position and the legs, so it has to be settled before the design freeze (S3), not after. Raising the hips and moving them 29 mm back is the only layout shown to get up with the legs colliding. Before it can be chosen it needs a walking check at that position and a packaging sketch with the shell opened for the hip brackets. Until then the stock layout stays the default, and for v1 a fallen robot is picked up by hand. The other routes to recovery (a tail-assisted roll or sit, or a different leg layout) have not been tried.

9.6Face, sound and touch

A flat vector chicken face on a black screen: big white eyes with dark pupils, small arched brows, an orange beak and pink cheeks. The beak stays fixed; the eyes, brows and accessories carry the expression. The face fills the upper two-thirds of the 320 × 480 portrait screen, and a slim status strip in the lower third shows battery, Wi-Fi and mode.

Curious, happy, surprised and love come from the renders; dizzy, confused, sleepy and error are first sketches in the same style. Event expressions (surprised, love) briefly override the mode's default.

Face rig. The face is drawn from LVGL shapes (circles, arcs, triangles), not stored bitmaps. Each expression is a preset of these parameters:

  • per eye: openness (0–1), scale, pupil offset, lid curve from open to the happy arc
  • brow angle on each side
  • beak opening (0–1)
  • cheek strength
  • accessory: none, heart, sparks, question mark, Zzz or spiral

Transitions tween over 150–300 ms. Blinks fire every 3–6 seconds at random. Gaze follows the turn and strafe commands. Small accessory icons may be images in PSRAM.

Stabilized eyes. Like a real chicken's head, the face holds still while the body moves. The whole face shifts opposite to torso roll (horizontally) and pitch (vertically) using the gravity vector from imu_task, low-pass filtered and clamped to about ±20 px. It is off when fallen or E-stopped. Only the face's bounding box redraws, at no more than 30 fps.

Sound. The onboard codec and speaker (to confirm on the 3.5") play short clips from LittleFS or SD through audio_task: a cluck when it starts walking and occasionally while walking (rate-limited, not every step), a startled squawk on a bump or fall, a happy cluck when petted and a sleepy coo on low battery or long idle. Volume and mute live in /settings. Sound must never affect control-loop timing.

Touch. A tap triggers love. A long press is an E-stop and turns torque off.

9.7Mode state machine

SIT. A scripted crouch onto the feet and tail, not a policy: joint targets interpolated over about 2 s at low gains, so v1 needs no extra training. It is entered from STAND on a sit command, after 5 s without a connection, on a servo over-temperature (S4) and as the first step of a low-battery stop (S5). WALK stops first, because the command goes to zero, and then sits. Standing up again goes back through STAND_INIT. SIT holds torque at low gains and drops to IDLE with torque off after a timeout, 30 s by default. The v1.1 SitStand policy replaces the script later.

Enable only when upright. STAND_INIT eases the legs to the home pose from wherever they happen to be, which is a bad idea with the robot on its back or held at an angle. IDLE to STAND_INIT is refused unless the gravity vector puts the torso within 20° of vertical (default, tunable). The robot answers the phone with a refusal and the reason, and the status strip shows it (S10).

  • It mounts on the comb's top boss; the comb is removed or reworked as the claw's cover.
  • Option A: a fixed gripper on top of the torso for carrying things handed to it. Simplest.
  • Option B: a one- or two-joint arm (shoulder pitch and gripper) that swings forward and down. Floor pickup needs a crouch-and-reach policy, like Microduck's GroundPick task with its beak.
  • v1 prepares for it with the shared boss, the mass reserve and payload randomization in training (§7.1).
  • Total mass stays at or below ~850 g; the XL330s are sized for Microduck's ~800 g.

13Open questions

  1. 3.5" FNK0104 details: exact pinout, touch controller and address, PSRAM size, 5 V input location, and whether the audio codec and amp match the 2.8".
  2. Which XL330 variant (M288 or M077) Pollen's actuator model targets. The constants file names only xl330 and BAM model m6, so ask Pollen or check the BAM parameters.
  3. Pollen's action post-processing: their runtime applies a low-pass to the leg targets while the training repo says policies are unfiltered (mdp.py, robotd). The 48-value proprioception block is settled: gyro, gravity, joint positions, joint velocities and last action.
  4. IMU choice. The ICM-42688-P is currently supply-constrained, with at least one breakout seller citing severe shortages and raised prices. Alternatives: LSM6DSOX or LSM6DSV16X with on-MCU fusion, or BNO085 with on-chip fusion (its I2C has known quirks on the ESP32).
  5. Where the tools run on the single Windows PC. MuJoCo Warp and mjlab are Linux-first, so training likely runs under WSL2. The twin and the firmware tooling can run natively on Windows or in WSL2, and each has a catch. WSL2's default NAT networking hides the twin's WebSocket server from the phone unless mirrored networking or port forwarding is set up. Flashing the ESP32 from WSL2 needs USB passthrough (usbipd-win), while a native ESP-IDF install flashes directly. Whether the BAM actuator model and the twin harness run on native Windows is unconfirmed.
  6. CAD tool for the physical design (H3). Decided: OpenSCAD for the parametric parts, with the foot toes done in hardware/ and the frame, bezel, comb and tail to follow the same pattern. Organic pieces such as the comb and tail feathers can be sketched elsewhere and exported to STL.
  7. Twin process layout: LVGL in the same process as the harness or alongside it.
  8. Battery choice and target runtime. It stays a 2S Li-ion pack, because the actuator model assumes 6.5–8.2 V at the servos.
  9. Reuse Microduck's leg STLs as they are, or redesign.
  10. Firmware language: C (default), C++ or Rust (esp-rs).
  11. Final designs for the dizzy, confused, sleepy and error faces.
  12. Servo firmware and setup tool. Fast Sync Read, which Microduck uses, needs XL330 firmware v46 or newer. Updating firmware and setting IDs is easiest with a USB-to-Dynamixel adapter such as the U2D2, which is not in the BOM yet.
  13. Hip position: keep Pollen's layout (cannot get up from the screen, but no change to the torso), or raise the hips 70 mm and move them about 29 mm back (gets up in simulation; needs the shell opened for the hip brackets, the battery and electronics repacked, the home pose re-trimmed and the walker retrained), or find another way to recover (§7.5).

15Notes for Claude Code

  • Phase A comes first. H0 bench work, early driver bring-up on one or two servos and fit-check prints may run in parallel. The full exit criteria of H1 to H3, and everything after them, wait for the design freeze (§3).
  • Work milestone by milestone and don't start the next until the exit criteria are met (§10).
  • Code in firmware/components/core never includes ESP-IDF headers. It reaches hardware only through the HAL (§8.1), and every core change must build and pass tests on the twin.
  • docs/sim2real-contract.md is the source of truth for observation and action layout. Firmware, twin and sim must reference the same contract hash.
  • docs/face-spec.md is the source of truth for expression presets and the state-to-expression mapping.
  • Never hand-edit generated policy headers; regenerate them with tools/policy_export.
  • Nothing in control_task blocks or allocates. The face and audio must never affect its timing.
  • Everything runs on the one Windows PC with the RTX 3060; there is no Mac. Training uses CUDA, likely under WSL2. Playback, the twin and firmware work share the same machine, and §13 covers native Windows versus WSL2.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop steps (wiring, soldering, flashing, gantry tests, weighing parts) are manual. Flag them for Neil rather than assuming results.
  • Record significant decisions as ADRs in docs/decisions/.
